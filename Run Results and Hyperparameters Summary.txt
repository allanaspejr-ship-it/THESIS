Metric Definitions
- Operational MAPE: percentage error computed only on rows above the low-load threshold; rows below the threshold are excluded to avoid unstable percentage errors near zero generation.
- MAE: average absolute MW error. Lower is better.
- RMSE: square-root of average squared MW error. It penalizes large hourly misses more strongly than MAE.
- R2: coefficient of determination compared with a mean-observed baseline. R2 near 1 is strong; R2 near 0 is similar to the mean baseline; negative R2 means the model is worse than that baseline for that split/plant.

Interpretation for RBFNN validation:
- R2 compares the model error against a simple mean-of-observed baseline. Negative R2 means the model errors are larger than that baseline for that split/plant, even when MAE, RMSE, or MAPE may still look operationally acceptable.
- Day-ahead evaluation is harder than one-step prediction because the model forecasts 24 hours recursively using only information available up to 23:00 of the previous day. It cannot use actual forecast-day generation, hydrologic variables, rainfall, Lake Lanao outflow, or actual outage status as inputs.
- Low or negative R2 is more likely when the test period has low variance in actual generation, sudden dispatch/outage changes, ramping events, or load/generation levels that differ from the validation pattern. R2 is sensitive to variance; if actual values stay in a narrow band, even moderate MW errors can produce poor R2.
- High errors are mainly caused by unobserved operational decisions and same-day physical constraints: dispatch instructions, forced outages, unit deratings, water availability changes, ramp limits, and hydrologic changes that are known only after the forecast is made.

Why Low R2 and High Errors Can Still Occur in a Fair Day-Ahead Setup
- The strict setup intentionally removes forecast-day actual information. That makes the results realistic but harder than one-step-ahead validation.
- Hydropower generation is influenced by operator dispatch, reservoir/water constraints, forced outages, deratings, and grid instructions. Some of these are not fully known at 23:00 before the forecast day.
- R2 is especially harsh when actual generation has low variability. If a plant operates near a stable level, the denominator of R2 is small, so a few MW of error can make R2 low or negative.
- MAPE can become high during lower generation periods because the same MW error is divided by a smaller actual value. This is why operational MAPE excludes rows below the low-load threshold, but high percentage errors can still occur near low-to-moderate generation.
- RMSE rises when the model misses sudden ramps or outage-related drops/spikes, because squared error emphasizes large misses.
- These causes do not automatically mean leakage or failed training. They usually indicate that day-ahead forecasting is being evaluated under realistic information limits.
